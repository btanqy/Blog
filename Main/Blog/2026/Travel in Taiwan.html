Points of Interest

Maokong



We got up here by car, in two way traffic through a road that was only one car wide. Also, there were plenty of people hiking up to Maokong, also on the same road. Though path seems pleasant, the heat and amount of mosquitos especially in greener areas like Maokong is just not worth it. However, there is plenty of wildlife along the road, including plenty of butterflies, lizards, and birds.


Most of the area is populated by tea farms and teahouses, so there isn't much else to do unless it's hiking. I did go to the Taipei Tea Promotion Center, which displays old machinery that are used for each step of the tea making process. They also have a nice garden in the back, but I didn't get to see it since it was raining - it went from sunny and beautiful to pouring in the span of half an hour. I think the most memorable experience was getting to sit down and talk to the tea farmer (I think volunteering) at the center, where they sat us down and let us try their signature Tieguanyin and Baozhong tea. We talked for about half an hour just chugging tea, learning about their process and leaves, and random stuff for half an hour before buying some of their tea leaves.


These is some of the best tea I've ever had. We learned that it's difficult to balance tea to get just the right amount of fragrance, ferment, dryness, and shape without having to steep the tea for too long to extract those qualities without the tea getting bitter. I got their Tieguanying and Baozhong teas, each one of the last few boxes of their award winning teas for the Taipei for $70. Since I bought directly from farmers I got a much better price than if I were to buy some in Taipei, and much worse, from America. I looked online and just the Baozhong itself was $110 online.

Shengkeng

Shengkeng doesn't really have much other than the old street, and is famous for their tofu. In fact, the entire street has a premium stank, mostly from stalls selling mala stinky tofu. However, it's not too far of a drive from Maokong and I think is pretty worth it for food, arts and crafts, and a few of shops specailizing in wooden items (pretty common everywhere else too though). When we got to Shengkeng, it was pouring. The worst part about rain in Taiwan isn't how wet you get, but how you get wet. Because of the climate, the rain is pretty much exactly at body temperature. Meaning I didn't realize I had a pool of water stuck in my shoes until I took them off later.

Raohe Night Market

Raohe Night Market is simply the superior night market. Compared to Shilin, the variety of food and ambeince has more character. Although there are tourist traps such as the stands selling cut fruit and grilled beef, there are a lot of good and classic finds as well. Since Raohe Night Market is very cramped, it is difficult to move around. However, there are some stalls where they have seating located behind them, giving you a chance to eat without having to move around for people to pass through.


I don't feel like many people know or think of going to the Keelung river, but it is both refreshing and relaxing to climb up and over the gate from Raohe Night Market to Keelung river. It's well lit and has many active people, even at night walking, biking, and running.

Dongmen Market

Dongmen Market is most definitely the locals' area. The majority of Dongmen Market is just traditional fare, such as fruits, vegetables, meat, fish, dried goods, and food. I ended up passing through most of it without buying anything, but there were some items that caught my eye, such as an impossibly huge lychee the size of a golfball. There were also many stands selling mysterious, dried seafood that had a stench so strong I had to vacate the vicinity immediately. There are also quite a lot of fruit stands, such as this one that has yellow watermelon for sale.

Personally, I didn't find much else to do in Dongmen asides from a nice bakery (Muhei Bakery, listed below) and a place that specializes in coffee flavored nougat crackers, called Jia Vin Bakery, which was unique and pretty decent.

Jioufen

Absolutely one of the biggest highlights of my trip. Jioufen is located decently far away and is perfect for a day trip, though the bus times and amount of tourists makes it difficult to get on the bus back to Taipei or arrive at the train station before the last train. Thankfully, my family drove me over, and I ended up staying a night in Jioufen. Since Jioufen is directly on the mountainside, there are quite a lot of stairs to get to and from place to place. However, the main walking area is decently straightforward to explore, as there are only a few streets to walk down before a dead end, or is led into another walking area. As most pictures online also show, all of Jioufen is covered in lanterns, which look great during the day but absolutely fantastic at night.


I think my favorite shot of the lanterns are when I turned my exposure down and really captured just the lanterns, especially when there were no tourists. In addition, it was super peaceful and quiet at this time. I was able to get this picture late at night, since most people had to take the last bus to Taipei and that most shops were closed by 9pm.


However, the most famous part of Jioufen is the Amei Teahouse. There were an unbelievable amount of tourists all flocking the same area, just to take a picture of the teahouse. There were way too many people to get a good picture, so here's what I have at best.


Although most of it is also tourist price, as an American with American expectations there were some really cool shops selling trinkets, snacks, ceramics, and wooden stuff at very affordable prices, which stretches the walking time through Jioufen significantly, despite the area being quite small.

Although staying a night at Jioufen was quite nice, there wasn't much to do since most shops weren't open late or early. I made the poor decision of deciding to hike the Mount Keelung Trail in the morning. I thought I was fit enough to take on this trail since it wasn't that long of a hike, but I was so unbelievably wrong. Going up, it was just stairs after stairs after stairs on uneven stone slabs.


Not only was the hike miserable, but it was already scarily hot by 9am, I think around 30C already. On top of that, there was zero clouds, no wind, and the sun beating down on my face the entire hike. Not to mention it was high enough altitude. I managed to climb all the way to the first observation deck, but wasn't able to reach to top one before I got concerningly dizzy, either from heatstroke or dehydration (I forgot to bring water and was sweating buckets). The views were still pretty good though. Here's one looking towards north shore:


and one looking back down at Jioufen:


I would definitely attempt to go hiking again in the future, but never again in this heat. I hear that winter in Taiwan is like a normal summertime here in the states, which is a lot more bearable. Next time, I'd definitely go the full route to get to the main observation deck, which has an incredible 360 view at the top.

Yingge Old Street

Yingge is known for their ceramics. Many people make their crafts from Yingge and are sold to shops in Yingge Old Street for resale. Though professional at first glance, many ceramics are actually factory made, from China, or are made by lesser known (not that reputable) craftsmen in the area. Although you can certainly find good stores which will also consequently have a pretty high price for their ceramics, I suggest looking at Ikkemto.



Also yes I see the no photography sign, and yes I did get permission to take pictures. The shopowners are personally acquainted with each of the master craftsmen making these ceramics. I pointed to one that I liked, and the shopowners were able to tell me everything about the lady who made it and how it was made. They also told me that real, local ceramics from these craftsmen are a dying art and will no longer sell someday, and that this store will eventually close as these older master craftsmen no longer make ceramics. This was a true gem to find.

However, that's not to say there aren't lots of other shops worth looking at. I highly recommend finding a red clay teapot. The one I found was roughly $7.50, though teapots approaching $120 per pot is also fairly common. Gai Wan is also a very popular method of brewing tea, where you put a lid over a pot to steep tea, available to purchase at many locations too, such as here.


It is also worth walking over the the Yingge Ceramics Museum. There are a lot of methods of shaping, firing, and glazing clay. The museum boasts a snake kiln, originally using wood to fire clay. They also have several different types of ceramics showing each type of firing and glazing on tiles to show comparisons. Beitou

Beitou is Taipei's main hot spring destination. There's a path going from the subway station up to Thermal Valley.


Along the way, is the Beitou Library (which was ok), Beitou Hot Spring Museum, and a nice walkway along the hot spring stream. I kind of regret not hitting the Beitou Hot Spring Museum, but it had a super long line to get in at the time. I didn't find much else to do in the area, but there were plenty of hotels and public hot springs that I didn't go to (I don't like hot springs).

Shilin

Shilin is the main night market when people think of night markets. All in all, Shilin is an enjoyable walk through carnival games, clothes shops, claw machines, and an unfathomable amount of food. At the very start of the night market, there were a lot of people lining up for Hot Star Fried Chicken, which isn't my favorite but is indeed a gigantic piece of fried chicken.


Walking through Shilin Night Market is pretty crazy at times, because they allow motorcycles to pass through. There is probably enough space for two motorcycles of width between stalls maximum, but most of the time, it's packed to the brim with people that motorcyclists really will nearly run you over and force you to the side just to get through.


However, there are lots of classic eats such as mini dumplings, taiwanese sausage, quail egg, cane sugar juice, tea, and grilled skewers that are very affordable and look delicious.

Zhongzheng

Zhongzheng is just a district within Taipei, but some things are spread out enough that I have it just included under Zhongzheng. Above is the Chang Kai Shek memorial hall, a massive and beautifully maintained ground dedicated to a dictator.


The left image is a closer image of the gate, while the right image is in front of the memorial hall where the soldiers come out to do a color guard show at the top of every hour.

Tamsui

Tamsui is the last stop for the metro red line, and is a popular destination especially among locals. The main attraction is just a strip along the river, leading out to the ocean that has snacks, trinkets, games, and food. Though I don't care for it, the huge ice cream tower was pretty unique from this spot. Across from Tamsui is Bali, a less visited, but equally nice area. My family and I ended up biking across the bridge from Tamsui to Bali, where there was some amount of biking on the road before we got to a really nice bike path leading all the way to the old street.


Bali Old Street is legitimately tiny. There are maybe 10 shops total and takes 1 minute to walk through it, so in my opinion, there's not much to see. However, my family likes this mystery item to this day I still have no clue what it could be.


The bike path leads directly to the ferry that goes to and from Bali and Tamsui, and surprise, takes Yoyo card! It was super convenient to cross the river, and it was mediocore mostly because the smell and noise was definitely noticeable on the ferry.

Taichung

Taichung feels a lot more local than Taipei - it's more quiet, more hot, and has significantly less pedestrian sidewalks. Whereas in Taipei, it's much easier to get around by any means, most people in Taichung tend to drive. Asides from some very good food spots, the only interesting thing that we planned was going to Miyahara, a confectionary store with some amazing atmosphere.

Alishan

This was the best part of my entire trip. Alishan mountain is known for its sunrise over the sea of clouds, which only happens in very specific weather conditions. Apparently, the best time to see it is when the weather is most unstable, or otherwise, right before a hurricane hits. Unforunately, I didn't get to see the sea of clouds, but this is the closest thing to it I took a picture of, which was during sunset at the hotel we were staying at.


Going to see the sunrise is somewhat tricky to plan at Alishan. Tickets to take the Zhushan train up to the peak of Alishan can be acquired online, though difficult to find. However, there were also many people waiting to buy tickets in person right before the train left. Also, no one knows when the train will depart until the afternoon before sunrise. In our case, the conductors decided to leave at 4:30am, which means we arrived and found one of the last parking spots close to 3:30am near the station. After riding the train up, there are two spots too see the sunrise: Zhushan Observation Deck and Xiaoliyuanshan Lookout. Zhushan Observation Deck tends to be crowded, but Xiaoliyuanshuan Lookout is a bit of a hike. In my opinion, Xiaoliyuanshan Lookout is far more worth it so long as you can walk quicker than all the other tourists to get a good spot. We waited another 15 minutes for the sunrise after arriving.


Although you can take the train back down, I was super grateful to have decided to just take the train up and walk back down. Most of the tourists miss out on the amazing scenery that Alishan has to offer. The paths are super comfortable, paved roads as well (up until you hit more stone stairs, but otherwise its great).


My favorite two spots for taking pictures were along the stairs and in front of the waterfall:



The path also loops around to many other points of interest, including my favorite which was the elder sister lake. Although you can't go onto the deck on the lake, it is still incredibly beautiful from all angles. There's also an plethora of tadpoles swimming in the lake too!


Tainan

Tainan a lot more slower paced and relaxed compared to Taipei. It is also much hotter and miserable in terms of weather, especially how it goes from sunny to pouring in as quick as 30 minutes without warning. However, Tainan does have beautiful park, pictured above. I also did visit the Tainan Cultural and Creative Park, only to find a few stands selling grilled fried chicken along with a cute, but overexpensive shop selling souvenirs. For a "Cultural and Creative" park, I didn't really get it and left feeling confused about how little there was to see there. I also went to the Asakusa shopping area, again, relatively small but still enjoyable.




Going down the street next to the Asakusa shopping area along Gouhua road was pretty enjoyable though, as there were tons of shops to look and gawk at, and of course, loads of food. I do regret not hitting Shennong Street, which has a lot more to see and is a good bit more walkable than literally being on the road down Gouhua. Another regret is actually hitting Snail Alley, which is apparently one of the more visited areas in Tainan famous for its snail themed neighborhood. Overall Snail Alley was really boring. We were lucky to go see the Tainan Garden Night Market, which is only open Thursday through Sunday.


Apart from standard night market fare, I did see a huge line for a lu wei (braised food) stand that I didn't bother waiting for. I also saw a stand selling crocodile meat, snake meat, and some other questionable meats too.


I was also very dissapointed of not being able to go see Anping Fort and the Anping Treehouse, since it was raining fairly heavily. The rain did let up by the time we reached the Tainan Salt Field Museum, run by an old lady volunteering out at the salt fields. There aren't very many visitors in this area anymore, and all the housing nearby is abandoned since salt farming is incomparable to the output of salt that can be mined and exported to Taiwan from China, making local salt farming completely unfeasible. Nowadays, the salt fields are just there for educational purposes, and people can sometimes go out to rake up salt and pile them up into mounds for fun. Back in the day though, salt farmers would fill and drain these fields to harvest salt and dump it into a conveniently placed railway system to process further. Additionally, the water used to flush or drain the fields were highly saline, which was also collected at an outlet where a salt processing factory extracted the salt out too.


Very close to the salt fields is also the Sicao Green Tunnel, a waterway with Amazon-like trees hanging above. There are boats available for you to take about every half hour, or as soon as the queue fills up to ride. I managed to get the second best seat, at the very back for some completely unobstructed views.


And yes, these trees are VERY low to the water. The tour guide and skipper had to work together to announce "DUCK LEFT" or "DUCK RIGHT" everytime we passed a low hanging tree, which was about every 30 seconds. In the picture below one of the people in front of me did end up getting hit by a tree.


After the boat ride, we visited the local temple and marine museum, which had a baby whale skeleton. There's not much else to see around here except for a few seafood restaurants, which mostly makes sense since this place is pretty much nowhere if not for the tunnel.


Yilan

Yilan is famous for their hot springs. The one pictured above is a public hot spring foot bath, where there are 4 different pools. The middle well contains scalding hot water that occasionally pours over into the bath. There were quite a few people thoroughly enjoying the bath, but I stood in the same area and felt like my skin was actively being burnt off of me. My feet hurt for the next few hours, but no one else seemed to have my issue...

There's also a roasted chicken shop that has custom kilns for baking chicken. It has pull apart chicken (meaning you put on gloves and literally pull it apart) paired with a plethora of sauces available.


This is some fantastic chicken available in Taiwan, but I would say there are better (but near impossible to find) places that sell fresh, free-range chicken that are a step above this one.

Ximending

Ximending is the considered the young night life area, though I did notice a significant lack of night life at all close to midnight. There are a lot of fun shops to see, such as Pop Mart, Uniqlo, and stores selling classic snacks. I especially like the Starbucks there, which is 3 stories tall and has super comfortable seating. There was also a huge line for Xing Fu Tang, which is famous for their boba tea.


Biking along Tamsui River outside Ximending was absolutely worth it though. There is a super nice, paved bike path all along the river, leading up the Dadaocheng Wharf. Though it was closed when we arrived, being able to bike with amazing views was incredible.



Zhongshan

The Taipei Fine Arts Museum was definitely one of the weirder experiences during this trip, but I'm really grateful to have gone here. It is a three story useum with only modern art. There are a lot of very odd and interesting exhibits, including the picture above where there wall is being painted in real time with a machine.


The nozzle moves around on two beams like a 3d printer, and spritzes the painting with one paint at a time. Although these two sculptures are more tame, there are some very wacky and non-postable art that were also very cool, including one which was a movie made to feel like a fever dream.

Food

Taiwan is famous for its food, and my Taiwanese relatives also agree that the only thing to do in Taiwan is eat food. All prices reflect everything that was ordered in the picture, with only a couple exceptions. Anything that is rated 6/10 or more, I liked it. Anything that is 8/10 or over, I thought was definitely worth it.

Yaoyue Teahouse


Rating: 6/10 | Price: $15-$24 | Location: Yaoyue Teahouse @ Maokong, Taipei
The food is pretty standard, nothing to write home about for the egg dish with tieguanyin leaves, edamame, stir friend squid with pork, or tofu. The tieguanyin sausage had an odd stewed pork flavor mixed with tea, whilst still having the essence of a taiwanese sausage.

Tieguanyin Tea Set


Rating: 6/10 | Price: $11 + $3pp | Location: Yaoyue Teahouse @ Maokong, Taipei
 Although it was great to experience how to make tea (pouring water into pot, and pot into pouring cup, and pouring cup into normal cup), the traditional tieguanyin leaves we ordered didn't really taste fragrant, and had a somewhat bitter aquafina-type aftertaste that I didn't enjoy. Take the time to travel to the Taiwan Tea Promotion Center instead, where they'll talk to you extensively about tea (we were there for like at least half an hour), offer lots of samples, and be able to buy quality, award-winning tea you won't find anywhere else in Taipei.

Tofu Ice Cream


Rating: 8/10 | Price: $1.70 | Location: 陳記豆腐養生恬點 @ Shengkeng, Taipei
If you're in Shengkeng, its worth the experience, but it tastes exactly what you expect it to be.

Central Market Seafood

Rating: 6/10 | Price: $18-30 | Location: Central Market Stir-Fry @ Zhongshan, Taipei

We saw a lot of what I can only assume are office workers that came here to eat and drink for dinner. Part of our order included shrimp and vegetable stir fry, squid and pork stir fry, and pork blood cake. It's very local and the food is decent!

Pepper Buns


Rating: 9/10 | Price: $2.10 | Location: Fuzhou Pepper Buns @ Raohe Night Market, Taipei
This place is very busy, and really is the only stall in all of Raohe that has a line. We got lucky and got our food in about 5 minutes. This place does indeed live up to the hype. It is crispy, fresh, hot, steamy, and peppery with a hint of sweetness from the onions. This is absolutely a must in Raohe Night Market.

Guabao


Rating: 8/10 | Price: $2.60 | Location: 林媽媽刈包 @ Raohe Night Market, Taipei
The taste and texture of the bun is very similar to a steamed bun, and contains two fatty pieces of braised pork, cilantro, peanuts, mustard greens, and some chili oil.

Peanut Ice Cream Roll


Rating: 10/10 | Price: $2.20 | Location: Raohe Night Market, Taipei
The roll has the consistencey and taste like a thin crepe, which is then filled with lots of peanut bits, cilantro, and two scoops of ice cream. None of this should work together but it actually tastes incredible - it is sweet, salty, and super fresh. This is also a must try in Raohe Night Market.

Street Corn


Rating: 2/10 | Price: $2.70 | Location: Raohe Night Market, Taipei
The flavors are nice, and is similar to popcorn chicken seasoning. However, the corn is dryer than beef jerky, while the cob has the consistency of beef jerky. Yes, the cob itself has somehow cooked to the point that I bit through and ate it without noticing.

Egg wrapped in Fishcake


Rating: 3/10 | Price: 5 sticks for $3.30 | Location: Raohe Night Market, Taipei
This is a quarter of a quail egg wrapped with fishcake, deep fried. It's very oily since it's soaked in oil for quite a while.

Tofu Pudding with Boba, Kidney Beans, Grass Jelly, Ai-yu


Rating: 4/10 | Price: $2.70 | Location: 鄉淳布丁豆花 @ Raohe Night Market, Taipei
Nothing special. The ice above the tofu pudding was very coarse.

Thick Shaobing with Egg, Thin Shaobing with Youtiao, Soy Drink, Peanut Rice Drink, Turnip Pastry



Rating: 9/10 | Price: $3-5 | Location: Fuhang Soy Milk @ Zhongzheng, Taipei
The shaobings are both phenomenal, but I prefer the thin one so it tastes less bready. Shaobing is pretty common in Taipei, so these items are slightly higher quality than a lot of already available competition, but people love to line up for it. I was in line for an hour. I also got the soy milk (pretty standard) and peanut rice drink, which is sweet and decently starchy due to the rice. It's kind of like drinking peanut butter, in a good way. I've never had it before trying it at Fuhang, but their turnip pastry is amazing. Savory, shredded bits of turnip paired perfectly with the flaky package. I didn't get to try it this time, but Fuhang is also famous for their salty soy milk which contains shrimp.

Pork Sandwich and Black Tea


Rating: 5/10 | Price: $1.70 | Location: Mos Burger @ Everywhere
Pork sandwich was kind of disappointing, but filling. However, the black tea is amazing. I didn't expect black tea from a fast food place to be so good, and if it weren't for the plethora of drinks available anywhere I would most definitely come back for the tea.

Meat-stuffed Tofu and Dongfen Noodles


Rating: 9/10 | Price: $2.30 | Location: Fishball Bonzai @ Jioufen, New Taipei City
This is unique to Jioufen and can't be found anywhere else. The tofu is stuffed with spicy meat, and sealed with fishcake and simmered. It has a slightly sweet sauce and is decently spicy. The dongfen noodles are also very bouncy and are fragrant because of the onions, green onions, and red garlic.

Conveyer Belt Sushi






Rating: 7/10 | Price: $40 | Location: Sushiro @ Zhongshan, Taipei
Very solid, fresh sushi. However, they don't have an english menu available. The price doesn't reflect the images above - we ordered much more than this.

Beef Bourginoun, French Tea, Chocolate Latte, and Fruit Layer Cake UPDATE LOCATION



Rating: 5/10 | Price: $23 | Location: Mr. Chu @ Yonghe, New Taipei City
This is a cat cafe, so the higher prices are to be expected. The drinks and food didn't feel particularly special, but were still delicious. The cake had crepe-like layers, with kiwi, dragonfruit, orange, and banana. I would recommend trying somewhere else.

Taro, Sweet Potato, and Mung Bean Balls


Rating: 9/10 | Price: $2.00 | Location: A Gan Yi Taro Ball @ Jioufen, New Taipei City
These are significantly difference in nearly every single way compared to other stores. The balls are incredibly bouncy, but in a way that seems closer to something like a chewy jelly, compared to other balls that always that are starchy and gritty. Nesting below the balls is coarse ice, mung beans, and red beans.

Jin Xuan Tea and Tea Snacks



Rating: 8/10 | Price: $20 + $3.30pp for tea, ~$3.30 per snack | Location: A-Mei Teahouse @ Jioufen, New Taipei City
The Jin Xuan tea is light and refreshing, but consuming too much can cause an upset stomach which is why tea snacks must be eaten along with the tea. The tea also smells like butter and does have hints of butter in taste for the first two steeps. The tea snacks we had were dried squid, edamame, and mung bean cake. The mung bean cake is spectacular - it is very sweet, sandy (which cakes the roof of your mouth), and has a pretty negligible amount of red bean inside. The cake pairs super well with tea.

Chocolate Latte


Rating: 6/10 | Price: $5 | Location: Coffee Law @ Da'an, Taipei
Solid chocolate milk! Ambience is fantastic. They are known for their watermelon coffee, but I don't drink coffee unfortunately.

Steamed Meat Bun


Rating: 9/10 | Price: $0.50 | Location: Yilan Gold Medal Buns @ Yingge Old Street, New Taipei City
The most freshly steamed meat buns served ever. I had to wait 20 minutes before attempting to eat it. The buns are perfectly pillowly, lightly sweet, and has a peppery filling. The inside also holds a lot of soup/meat juice.

Lemon Ai-yu Drink


Rating: 5/10 | Price: <$1 | Location: Yingge Old Street, New Taipei City
Kind of like a lemonade, but with tasteless jelly inside.

"Sushi" and Garlic Noodles


Rating: 3/10 | Price: $10 | Location: 阿婆壽司 @ Yingge Old Street, New Taipei City
This isn't poorly rated because the food tasted bad, but rather, it was an odd combination and confusing eating experience. First off, the tofu bags are surprisingly sweet. I usually love eating tofu bags but these are insanely sweet. The roll wrapped in egg contains rice, cucumber, mayo, pork floss, and ham. This seriously shouldn't work, and it almost does. The mayo is just way too sweet. The seaweed roll contains rice, pork floss, and some kind of crunchy melon. This was my favorite here. The garlic noodles were insanely garlicky - I thought it was super spicy when I first tasted it, only to find out it was just pure garlic flavor. Apparently, it's made with red garlic which gives it its unique kick. Alongside the noodles came some mushroom and a century egg, both had no flavor.

Taro Duck


Rating: 7/10 | Price: $13 | Location: Tsui Feng Yuan @ Songshan, Taipei City
I found this place because it had a Michelin recommendation. When I arrived, it was only elderly eating here, at which point I knew this was going to be good. Additionally, while I was waiting for my takeout, the waiter was super nice and gave me a cup of tea. The dish contains a thin layer of duck, covered in taro paste, then deep fried. Usually, this dish takes more than a day to prepare, and the price reflects the work needed to put into it. It is crunchy, savory, and to be honest, a bit too oily, but is otherwise a fantastic concept and delicious. It comes with some cabbage and dipping sauce. The dipping sauce has no flavor.

Grilled Fried Chicken


Rating: 4-9/10| Price: $3 | Location: 維縈家鄉碳烤雞排 @ Various Locations
Taiwan is also famous for frying a chicken cutlet, smothering it in a sweet sauce, then grilling the whole thing for some char. There are quite a few stores, but I think the best one is the stand outside of Shilin Night Market, where there's always a line and the chicken is both fresh and crispy. The chicken does have cartilage and bones when it was fried, so there is some expertise needed eating around them. Also, the amount of chicken they give is pretty insane. I also did try another location, and is excessively sweet and slightly bland.

Breakfast Sandwiches


Rating: 4/10 | Price: $4.70 | Location: My Warm Day @ Zhongzheng, Taipei
I was craving some american food and was dissapointed to find out these sandwiches were sweet. The pancake sandwich (egg, hashbrown) tasted like pound cake, and the danish sandwich (tomato, egg, mayo, bacon) was at least better but still surprisingly sweet.

Smoked Chicken Bread and Garlic Danish


Rating: 4/10 | Price: $2.80 | Location: Sunmerry @ Da'an, Taipei
Solid, but still too sweet. Garlic bread was very garlicky and had good texture though!

Almond Berry Croissant



Rating: 9/10 | Price: $4.80 | Location: Muhei Bakery @ Da'an, Taipei
This bakery has both regular (butter, chocolate, etc) croissants and very odd (matcha, peanut, crab) croissants. The almond berry croissant had the perfect bready-flaky consistency, and was filled with chocolate and jam inside, but somehow wasn't overly sweet. I loved every bite of it! There was also a nice seating area inside, but I didn't stick around.

Cafeteria Food


Rating: 1/10 | Price: $7 | Location: Just don't go
There are quite a few locals, but the food is just so unbelievably subpar. The fried food spareribs were soggy, the fish was fishy, the vegetables and tofu were at least standard. However, cleanliness is horrific. One of the ladies that ladled food onto our tray dropped a sparerib on the counter, the one that had everyone's trays and hands sliding across it, and she put it back into the sparerib container. Actually disgusting.

Lemon Cake

Transportation and Stays

I think I chose the stupidest flight to get to Taiwan. There are many, albeit more expensive, direct flights from SFO to TPE (Taoyuan), which sat between $1200 to $1500 at the time. There were also quite a few layovers I was interested in, like a 20 hour layover in Seoul or Osaka. I ended up going with two layovers, with my first flight landing at Honolulu, and the next flight landing at Guam before getting to Taiwan. My cost was close to $1200 for these two layovers.

On the first leg to Hawaii, I really expected United Airlines to feed me at some point, especially since it was a 6 hour flight from 7pm to 10pm (California to Hawaii time), and I just ended up staying hungry for the whole flight. I'm not sure why, but this specific plane felt a lot more cramped and uncomfortable, even compared to the flights also by United Airlines to Guam and Taipei. At least the view is nice.


Flying into Guam, I was pleasantly surprised and treated with two meals, which makes more sense since it is almost an 8 hour flight from 2pm to 6pm (Hawaii to Guam time). I don't quite remember what the first meal was, but I do distinctly remember that the second meal was two, cold turkey sandwiches that were LEGENDARY. Not that it tasted good, it just tasted like my childhood. Bare minimum, basic, turkey slice, cheese, bread. That was it. Flying into Guam, I did notice that the transition from island paradise to island semi-kind-of-not-very-paradise was drastic.


I also still don't know what this swirly thing in the ocean is when we flew over Guam...


Flying from Guam to Taipei, I enjoyed another two meals, which surprised me the most since it was a 4 hour flight from 8am to 10am (Guam to Taipei time). They gave us meat noodles for the first meal, and I chose to get bread pudding for the second meal. That bread pudding was honestly pretty solid for airplane food. Arriving at Taipei honestly looks pretty similar to Guam:


Getting to and from Taipei from Taoyuan airport is fairly easy. After coming out, the airport terminal goes directly to Taipei Main Station for about $5, and in my opinion is the quickest option without a car. All things transportation asides from taxi (sometimes) take yoyo card, which is the local transit card. Theoretically it should cost around $3 to get the card itself, but at the airport the machine tried to charge me $15. I assume it probably was going to come preloaded, but I ended up not getting it. Going around Taipei via the local metro is also very easy. Though it can be somewhat packed during rush hour, I don't really ever find an issue with people and just say excuse me to pass through. There are also booths, both digital and operator to help with directions and refilling yoyo cards.


As you can see, it's mostly chill and very clean on the metro. Cost is variable, but is usually between $1.50 and $2.50 per ride.




I did end up getting the monthly pass though, which was around $40, covering metro, bus, and bikes (up to 30 minutes per ride). For the bus, rides are significantly cheaper though waiting for buses is always slightly slower. Though the actual difference in time waiting isn't too large, the heat makes it much more noticeable. There are a mix of gas electric buses and buses are slowly being converted to electric, and are maintained fairly well.


I thought it was pretty funny to see that some buses now have charging ports too.


Lastly, the hidden gem is biking. There are an unbelievable amount of bike rental stations called Youbikes, and offer both manual biking and e-bikes. There have only been a handful of times where I couldn't find a spot to park or couldn't find a bike at a certain station, but then I'd be able to walk a couple minutes to the next station and have no issue. Along with my monthly pass, I can rent for up to 30 minutes free. So long as I rent, park within 30 minutes, and rent again from a separate station, I can bike as far as I want for free. Youbike does have a funny system where you cannot rent a bike from the same station for a buffer of 15 minutes I believe. But if you do need to park without a station, you can simply tap the yoyo card and lock the bike with a pin in the basket so it can't be stolen. Another thing I'm excited to rave about are the e-bikes. For city riding, they are life changing. The acceleration is fantastic, and most bikes are already charged up to 80% and ready to go. However, they do have some speed limitation and stop helping with acceleration past somewhere around 15mph. Cost for the regular bikes are $0.50 per half hour, and $1 per half hour for the e-bikes.


Though there are a lot of dedicated and beautiful bike paths like the one on the left, there are also a distinct lack of paths or regard to bikes on the road. Since streets are small in Taipei, cars have to weave around bicyclists and aren't very likely to move for you. My cousin almost got flattened by a car once.




For a shorter trip, not having a car is no issue. However, going into the mountains or east side of Taiwan will be more difficult by bus and local train, as attractions can be more distant from each other. Additionally, it will be hard to find some hidden gems such as teahouses, shops, plantations, etc. along the way.

For my stay in Guam, I ended up staying at Hotel Tano in Tumon. Honestly, the location is totally fine, I just think that transportation in Guam is hellish everywhere. Since I didn't order a Stroll (Uber equivalent on Guam), I just walked from the airport to Hotel Tano. You aren't supposed to do that. There are no sidewalks, the crosswalks are out of order, and the roads lead you through a jungle packed with wild dogs and trash before you get to the more walkable, tourist area. Anyway... Hotel Tano is pretty ok for $105, and is located near most of the food options in Tumon.


For Jioufen, I ended up staying at Nostalgic Inn for $25. I thought it would be super sketchy for the location and price, but it was actually fantastic. There was nice lobby area, large hangout area, and comfortable rooms with AMAZING air conditioning in the room. The location was just a minute away from Jioufen Old Street too! I think the only thing that threw me off was how the host gave me an electronic scent dispenser with mosquito repellant, and warned me there are a lot of mosquitos in the area. I plugged it into an outlet the entire stay, and didn't have any issues getting bit.




The bathroom was pretty small, but I don't have any big complaints about it.


My stay in Alishan was something I'd consider "nice-barebones". The room is very pleasant and has all your basic needs, but there's not much more than that available at this hotel. Though free breakfast is offered, most people are here to see the sunrise and aren't able to arrive back in time for it. There's an overpriced convenience store available as well, especially since this place is 15 minutes out by car from any other building. Though there are nice views, the Alishan Youth Activity Center is a somewhat solid option for $120.


My favorite stay was in Tainan at a BNB called De Provintia Straat. It is both a hostel and a hotel. For the three of us, we got our own room and bathroom for $90. The host was incredibly friendly and offered us complimentary fruits, snacks, and cut up some mango and left it in the communal fridge for us later. That was the best mango I've ever had in my life, it tasted like straight up candy. I'll let the pictures speak for the room itself, but apart from what you can see, the place includes an electric kettle, tea, coffee, soaps, shampoo, floss, and bath bombs.







I also can't believe that we have access to a full kitchen and laundry too!
